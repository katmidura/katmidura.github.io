WellPal — Supporting mental well-being through everyday routines

Overview
WellPal is a personal well-being gadget concept designed to make emotional support more approachable for adults aged 50+. Rather than framing mental health as a separate clinical task, it integrates guided breathing, reflection, and reliable resources into familiar daily routines. As an individual project, I led the desk research, user interviews, iterative prototyping, and physical Arduino prototype development.
Role
Product Research & Concept Development
Timeline

Tools

01 — Context

Everyday routines as an entry point to well-being

Attitudes toward mental health often vary across generations, with stigma and reluctance to seek professional help remaining common among older adults. While exploring how technology might lower this barrier for adults aged 50+, desk research pointed to a clear pattern: everyday household routines like cleaning and organizing naturally encourage calmness and a sense of control, while trust in new technology depends heavily on understanding its practical purpose.

02 — Problem

Making emotional support feel natural, not clinical

For many adults aged 50+, talking about emotions or reaching out to a specialist feels uncomfortable or unnecessary. Existing mindfulness and mental health tools often add another task to a person's schedule or rely on clinical framing that creates resistance. The project set out to answer how everyday activities could become a low-pressure opportunity to care for mental well-being without feeling like therapy.

03 — Process

From a smart vacuum to a flexible companion

To understand attitudes toward emotional self-care and technology, I interviewed two adults aged 50+ (from Poland and the Netherlands) alongside desk research on generational mental health awareness. Because the interview sample was small, I treated the findings as early directional insights rather than representative data, using them to guide three rapid concept prototypes tested with fellow students.

Decision 1 — supporting self-reflection before conversation: Interviewees noted they prefer processing difficult emotions on their own before speaking with someone close. Instead of pushing users toward external help immediately, I focused the concept on private, self-paced reflection and trustworthy mental health information.

Decision 2 — detaching the assistant from a single chore: My initial concept was an AI-powered vacuum cleaner that guided breathing exercises during cleaning, alongside two other chore-based explorations (an immersive dishwashing installation and a movement game). Student feedback showed that tying the assistant to one appliance was too restrictive—and wearing earbuds felt inconvenient. I pivoted to a standalone, detachable gadget usable across different daily routines or on its own.

Decision 3 — framing the product as a practical gadget, not a therapy tool: Using the Business Model Canvas to define long-term value, I positioned WellPal as a calm, practical everyday companion rather than a mindfulness or clinical device, avoiding the stigma that often deters older users from adopting mental health products.

04 — Solution

A tangible audio companion for daily calm

I built a working physical prototype featuring a wooden enclosure powered by an Arduino. Instead of a screen-heavy interface, the device uses physical interaction to play three pre-recorded audio scenarios covering different emotional themes. WellPal brings four core functions together into one approachable object:

  • Guided breathing and gentle movement prompts during daily tasks.
  • Structured audio reflection to help users organize their thoughts.
  • Low-pressure emotional support woven into existing routines.
  • Access to reliable mental health information without generic advice.

05 — Outcome

A validated physical concept ready for target-group testing

The project progressed from an initial smart-vacuum idea to a physical Arduino prototype demonstrated at the final Expo, where visitors of various ages interacted with the concept.

Because early prototype testing involved students rather than adults aged 50+, and the prototype used pre-recorded audio rather than live AI, the concept remains at the proof-of-concept stage. Future iterations would require testing directly with the 50+ target group and consulting psychologists, gerontologists, and AI specialists to validate both usability and therapeutic boundaries.
