Making crystallized honey easier to use — Designing a ring for honey jars

Overview:

Beekeepers face many different challenges, from maintaining their hives to selling their products. As a team, we wanted to find a problem that was shared by different beekeepers and that we could realistically address through design, without needing specialist knowledge. After speaking with beekeepers and learning more about their customers, we decided to focus on the problem of crystallized honey. We developed a concept of a special ring that heats a selected part of a honey jar to make the honey easier to scoop. My responsibilities included conducting one of the interviews, taking part in the analysis of all four interviews and creating the 3D models of the ring, jar and spoon in Blender.

Tags:

UX Research, Product Design, 3D Modeling

My role:

UX Researcher / 3D Modeler

Team:

4 UX/UI Designers

Timeline:

October 2025 – January 2026 (15 weeks)

Tools:

Google Forms, Blender

Context

Beekeeping involves more than taking care of bees and maintaining hives. Beekeepers also need to manage their products, find customers and sell their honey.

However, people working in this field can have very different experiences and needs. Professional beekeepers and hobbyists may face different challenges depending on their work and the size of their apiaries.

We wanted to understand these differences before deciding what problem to focus on.

Problem

Our main challenge was the variety of problems that beekeepers face. Some were related to maintaining hives, others to equipment, costs or selling products. Many of these issues were very specific and required specialist knowledge that we did not have. It was difficult to find a problem that was common among different beekeepers and that we could realistically address through design. We wanted to find a shared problem that was meaningful to beekeepers but also possible for our team to work on.

Exploration

Interviews

We started by conducting interviews with four people who were either professional beekeepers or bee hobbyists. I personally conducted one of the interviews and took part in analysing all four conversations together with the team. During the analysis, we identified nine different problems that appeared across the interviews or were mentioned by individual participants.

For each problem, we created three parts:

  • Observation: What we heard or noticed during the interviews.
  • Diagnosis: What we thought was causing the problem.
  • Recommendation: What could potentially help address it.

Some of the problems we identified included:

  • Relocating beehives.
  • External threats to bees.
  • Risk of swarming.
  • Beekeepers' clothing.
  • High prices of equipment.
  • Distrust towards cheap honey.
  • Time-consuming remelting processes.

Two topics appeared particularly often:

Finding customers: All four beekeepers mentioned difficulties with continuously finding customers.

Honey crystallization: Three out of four participants mentioned crystallization as an issue.

Narrowing down the problem

After mapping all nine problems, we realised that their variety was one of our biggest challenges. Many of the issues were important, but solving them would require specialist knowledge or a more specific approach than we could offer as a design team. We started looking for a more manageable direction. The interviews revealed two recurring topics: finding customers and crystallized honey. We decided to explore crystallization further because it connected the beekeepers' experiences with the way customers perceive and buy honey. We wanted to understand whether crystallization actually influenced consumers' purchasing decisions and whether there was an opportunity to make this type of honey easier to use.

Consumer Survey

We did not want to base our next decisions only on our assumptions, so we decided to ask consumers about their honey-buying habits and preferences. We collected 46 survey responses, but only 36 respondents said they bought honey. Therefore, we focused on these 36 people when analysing honey-related purchasing habits.

Our survey included questions about: How often people buy honey. Where they usually buy it. Whether they pay attention to its expiry date. How crystallized honey affects their purchase decisions. Whether they buy creamed honey. How they react to unusual honey colours. Whether they buy other bee products. What they consider important when choosing honey.

Key insights

  1. Crystallization can discourage customers from buying honey

    2. We asked how crystallized honey affected respondents' purchasing decisions.

    • 8.3% were more likely to buy crystallized honey.
    • 58.3% were indifferent to crystallization.
    • 33.3% said it discouraged them from buying it.

    This showed us that crystallization could be a barrier for some customers, even though most respondents did not consider it important.

  2. Many customers buy honey directly from beekeepers

    3. When asked where they usually buy honey, 55.6% selected beekeepers as one of their usual sources. Respondents could select up to two answers. We also found that most respondents bought honey only a few times a year:

    • 58.3% bought honey a few times a year.
    • 33.3% bought it less often than once a year.
    • 5.6% bought it once a month.
    • 2.8% bought it several times a month.

    These results made us consider whether a beekeeper could introduce our product directly to their existing customers.

  3. There is room to explore other bee products

    4. Around one in three honey buyers had also tried other products offered by beekeepers. This suggested another possible opportunity for beekeepers to expand their offer. However, we decided to focus on crystallized honey for our project.

Designing the Product

From insights to a product concept

Based on our research, we decided to focus on two main aspects:

  1. Making crystallized honey easier and more convenient to use.
  2. Exploring how a new product could be introduced through a beekeeper's existing customer base.

Our idea was to create a set consisting of a special honey jar and a matching ring. We imagined that a beekeeper could initially offer this set to their customers, introducing them to a different way of using crystallized honey. Our main question became: How could we make crystallized honey easier to scoop without heating the whole jar?

A ring for crystallized honey

We came up with the idea of a ring that could be placed around a specially designed honey jar. The ring would heat a selected part of the jar at the height where users usually scoop honey with a spoon. Instead of heating the whole jar, our concept focused on warming only the area needed to make scooping easier. The ring would not have direct contact with the honey.

How would it work?

We wanted the product to be simple to use, so we included a few basic controls and indicators:

  1. Power button: A button to start the heating process.
  2. Battery indicator: A row of dots showing the remaining battery level.
  3. Heating status: A small light indicator that would stay red while the ring was heating up and turn green once it reached the target temperature.

This would help users understand whether the honey was ready to be scooped without needing complicated controls.

A matching honey jar

We wanted it to have a more attractive appearance and create a recognisable set that a beekeeper could offer to their customers. We explored a cuboid shape for the jar. We thought it might also make scooping easier, although we did not test this assumption. The set was designed to include:

  1. A specially designed honey jar./li>
  2. A heating ring fitted around the jar.
  3. A long spoon designed to reach the honey inside.

We imagined that a beekeeper could offer the set directly to customers as a different way to present and use crystallized honey.

3D Modeling

From concept to 3D model

After developing the product concept, I was responsible for creating its 3D model in Blender. It was my first 3D modeling project, so I had to learn how to use the software while working on the design. I created models of the set and I also included dimensions of the different elements to show how they would fit together.

Final renders

I prepared renders showing the ring placed on the jar as well as the ring on its own. This allowed us to present the overall appearance of the product and show how the different elements of the set would look together. The project ended at the digital concept stage, so we did not create or test a physical prototype.

Outcome

As a result, we developed a concept of a heating ring designed to make crystallized honey easier to scoop. The project included research with beekeepers and consumers, an analysis of different problems, a consumer survey and a 3D concept of the ring and its matching jar. My main contribution to the final result was creating the 3D models and visualisations of the product in Blender. The concept explored one possible way to make crystallized honey more convenient to use and give beekeepers another product they could offer to their customers.

What could be improved?

There are several things we would need to explore further before the concept could become a real product:

  • Product testing: We did not create a physical prototype, so we could not check whether the ring heats the honey in the intended way.
  • Heating performance: The target temperature, heating time and battery life would need to be tested.
  • Usability: We would need to check whether the ring is easy to attach, use and remove from the jar.
  • Different jars: We would need to see whether the ring could work with different jar sizes or require a dedicated jar.
  • Customer feedback: We did not test whether customers would actually want to use or buy such a product.

Project Learnings

This project taught me how to work with a broad problem space and gradually narrow it down to a more specific design opportunity. I learned that problems that seem important to one group can have very different causes and may require knowledge outside our area of expertise. Finding a problem that is both meaningful and possible to address is an important part of the design process. I also learned how interviews and surveys can complement each other. Conversations with beekeepers helped us understand their challenges, while the survey gave us another perspective on how customers perceive and buy honey. Another important lesson was learning to connect research findings with a product idea without treating every assumption as a proven fact. Finally, I learned how to use Blender and create my first 3D product model, including its dimensions and visualisations. Looking back, I would continue the project by creating a physical prototype and testing it with both beekeepers and honey buyers. This would help us understand whether the ring actually makes crystallized honey easier to use and whether it could become a useful addition to a beekeeper's offer.
